# Business Economics Framework

The economics model exists to test whether the proposed way of creating and delivering value can plausibly produce the founder's desired business outcome.

Do not confuse a revenue forecast with economic viability.

## Start with the economic unit

Define the unit that makes sense for the model, for example:

- customer/account;
- employee processed;
- transaction;
- location;
- project;
- subscription;
- seat;
- order.

A business can have multiple relevant units. Choose the smallest set needed to understand cost and value behavior.

## Revenue mechanics

Understand:

- price/fee structure;
- billing frequency;
- implementation/setup revenue;
- usage/volume revenue;
- minimum commitments;
- discounts;
- revenue shares;
- upsells/expansion;
- refunds/credits;
- payment timing.

Separate a **pricing decision** from a **willingness-to-pay hypothesis**.

## Variable and semi-variable costs

Look for costs that scale with customers, volume, complexity, or exceptions:

- direct labor;
- third-party software/platform fees;
- payment/processing fees;
- commissions;
- partner/referral shares;
- implementation/onboarding labor;
- customer support;
- rework/error correction;
- compliance/filing costs;
- communications/infrastructure;
- insurance exposure where it meaningfully scales.

Do not classify recurring operational labor as fixed merely because it is currently performed by a salaried founder/employee.

## Contribution logic

Where useful:

```text
Revenue per unit
- variable/semi-variable delivery cost
- sales commissions/revenue shares attributable to the unit
- support/servicing cost attributable to the unit
= contribution before fixed overhead
```

Choose definitions appropriate to the business and label them clearly.

## Acquisition economics

Understand:

- sales/marketing cost;
- salesperson compensation;
- lead/referral cost;
- founder selling time when material;
- implementation/pre-sales effort;
- sales cycle;
- conversion rate assumptions.

Early relationship-driven sales may understate scalable acquisition cost.

## Retention and lifetime assumptions

If the model depends on recurring revenue, understand:

- churn/retention;
- contract duration;
- expansion/contraction;
- servicing cost over time;
- commission duration;
- switching behavior;
- customer concentration.

Do not use a lifetime-value calculation with arbitrary retention assumptions and then treat the result as evidence.

## Cash timing

Profitability and cash are different.

Probe:

- upfront hiring/build cost;
- implementation cost before revenue;
- billing/payment delay;
- payroll/vendor timing;
- deposits/prepayments;
- seasonality;
- refunds/chargebacks;
- tax payment timing;
- working capital requirements.

## Sensitivity

Identify a small number of assumptions that dominate the model.

Examples:

- price;
- sales commission percentage;
- customers per operator;
- onboarding hours;
- retention;
- sales conversion;
- support burden;
- error/rework rate.

Use scenario/range analysis when inputs are uncertain. Avoid false precision.

## Coherence questions

The economics are not sufficiently understood until the model can answer, at a useful level:

- What makes one additional customer/unit economically attractive or unattractive?
- Which cost grows fastest with scale?
- Which assumption most changes margin?
- What happens under a plausible downside scenario?
- When does cash become constrained?
- Which strategic decision should change if the economics do not hold?
